Genericization isn't
the problem.
It's the report card.

Hansika Bansal  ·  2026

At some point, someone asked a colleague to "just Xerox this" about a machine that wasn't a Xerox. Someone else told their friend to "Google it" on Safari. And somewhere in India, a carpenter asked for Fevicol when he meant any adhesive at all. Three brands. Three different decades. Three very different outcomes from what looks, on the surface, like the same problem.

Brand genericization is when your brand name stops referring to your product and starts referring to an entire category. Legally, it's supposed to be a nightmare. A brand that becomes generic loses its trademark, which means competitors can use your name freely. But the legal framing misses something important: genericization doesn't happen to weak brands. It happens to brands that won so completely that language had to make room for them.

The question isn't whether your name became a verb. The question is what you do when it does, and whether your brand is actually built for that kind of weight.

01  ·  Xerox
Winning the legal battle, losing the cultural one
Xerox Corporation  ·  Trademark Defense Advisory File: XRX-1974-ONGOING
Subject Brand genericization — "xerox" adopted as generic term for photocopying
Market position Monopoly 1959–1975. Competition entered 1970s. Trademark at risk by 1974.
Response filed Legal notices to publishers. "Don't say xerox, say Xerox" campaigns. Continuous enforcement.
Summary assessment

When Xerox released the Model 914 in 1959, plain paper photocopying simply didn't exist before them. They held a monopoly for nearly twenty years. By the time competition arrived in the 1970s, "xeroxing" was already in the dictionary and the trademark was under genuine threat.

The defense was methodical. Letters to publishers. Campaigns asking people to stop using the verb. Lawyers engaged continuously. Technically, it worked. The trademark held.

What the legal win didn't solve: Xerox isn't in the cultural conversation anymore. Nobody associates the brand with anything beyond a photocopier, a product category that is itself shrinking. The defense protected the name's legal status while the brand quietly became irrelevant. You can win the trademark argument and still lose the more important one.

Legal outcome TRADEMARK
RETAINED
Cultural outcome not found cultural relevance: nil

Trademarks don't become extinct because of language. They become extinct through neglect. Xerox chose legal vigilance over cultural relevance, and ended up with both. Just one matters more than the other now.

02  ·  Google
Knowing which battles aren't worth fighting
Google
to google something ⌕
About 8,410,000,000 results (0.48 seconds)
AI Overview

Google became a verb so fast there was barely time to resist it. By the early 2000s, the search engine had won its category so completely that no competitor could even claim the activity. "To google" entered Merriam-Webster and Oxford as a lowercase verb in 2006.

Google's response was to barely respond at all. They maintained trademark guidelines, didn't run public campaigns, didn't ask anyone to stop using the verb. This wasn't confidence for the sake of it. The moat was structural — data, infrastructure, scale accumulated over years in a way that made independent competition near-impossible. Microsoft had Bing. Safari had its default search. Neither came close.

But the moat wasn't untouchable. ChatGPT showed that people would use conversational AI to get answers instead of searching for links. Google didn't integrate Gemini because it could afford to be bold. It integrated Gemini because not doing so meant slowly becoming the thing people used before they switched to something better. The structural scale didn't stop the threat. It gave Google enough resources to see it coming and adapt before the gap became unrecoverable.

First known use: 2001. Googled them before the meeting. People still say "let me google that" even when they're on Safari, Bing, or asking Gemini. The verb detached from the product. That's the whole story.
G google.com › policies › trademark
Google Trademark — Use of Google Trademarks
Please don't use our name as a verb to refer to search using any other search engine. But also: we know nobody is googling things on another search engine to hurt us. The moat made legal defense largely unnecessary. The data and scale did the work instead.

The moat didn't make Google invincible. It made Google fast enough to survive the one threat that actually mattered. And the verb survived everything.

03  ·  Fevicol
When the name becomes the strategy
Pidilite Industries  ·  Since 1959

The idiom
outlasts
the trademark.

फेविकोल का जोड़

Fevicol launched in 1959, the same year as the Xerox 914. By the 1980s, it had replaced "gond" and "chipkane wali cheez" in everyday Hindi. Every adhesive became Fevicol. Pidilite faced the same genericization pressure as Xerox but made a different call: instead of fighting it, they built on top of it.

The Ogilvy campaigns from the 1990s onward never once explained how Fevicol worked. The bus ad shows passengers packed so tightly onto a rural bus that no physical force could separate them. A small Fevicol logo appears at the end. Neither ad sells adhesive. Both ads sell permanence.

What Pidilite understood is that "Fevicol ka jod" had already become a Hindi idiom for any bond that doesn't break. Instead of trying to claw the name back into trademark-safe territory, they made the idiom do brand work. The genericization wasn't a legal threat to manage. It was a positioning asset to deploy.

Emotional equity is harder to challenge than trademark protection. A competitor can file a legal brief. They can't file one against a cultural idiom that generations grew up with.

The variable nobody talks about

The brand name so inseparable from the category that when the crisis hit, it owned the crisis too.

Maggi had the same category ownership as Fevicol. In large parts of India, instant noodles were Maggi. The emotional equity was real and deep, built over decades of the same kind of cultural familiarity that made Fevicol untouchable.

Then the 2015 lead crisis hit. Every headline said "Maggi." Not "instant noodles." Not "noodle brands." Maggi. The same genericization that should have been a moat became the liability — the name was so inseparable from the category that the crisis owned the category too. Competitors benefited. The brand spent years recovering.

The difference between Maggi and Fevicol isn't emotional equity. It's replaceability. Fevicol is the adhesive — there is no functional substitute. Maggi was the emotional default in a category where physical substitutes existed and were ready the moment the brand stumbled.

Fevicol — irreplaceable Category ownership = moat. No substitute exists. Crisis couldn’t touch it.
Maggi — replaceable Category ownership = liability. Substitutes were ready. Crisis owned the category.

Category ownership protects you when you’re irreplaceable. It amplifies the crisis when you’re not.

Genericization is a report card with a footnote. The grade tells you how completely you won. The footnote says: make sure the thing you won can’t be replaced.
